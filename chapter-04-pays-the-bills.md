# Chapter 4: Software That Pays the Bills

There is an Excel spreadsheet somewhere in a mid-sized manufacturing company that, if it stopped working, would cause a crisis. Nobody remembers who created it. The original author retired seven years ago. It's been modified by at least fifteen different people, each adding their own sheet, their own macros, their own formulas that reference other formulas that reference lookup tables on hidden sheets.

It's a monster. It's critical business infrastructure. And it's not software, officially, so there's no backup plan, no documentation, no disaster recovery. It just runs, every day, calculating inventory levels or pricing models or something equally important.

This is what most software development actually looks like.

## The Unglamorous Truth

When people think about software development, they imagine creating the next Facebook or building revolutionary AI. The reality is that most programmers spend their days maintaining internal business systems that nobody outside the company will ever see.

We write software to track inventory. To process payroll. To manage customer relationships. To generate reports. To automate workflows. To integrate System A with System B because they were made by different vendors who hate each other.

This is the software that runs the world. Not social media. Not games. Not flashy consumer apps. Boring enterprise software that helps businesses do business things.

And it pays remarkably well, which is why most of us do it.

## The Fortune 500's Tech Stack

Every large company runs on a combination of:

**SAP or Oracle** for enterprise resource planning. These systems cost millions to license, millions to implement, and millions to maintain. They're powerful, complex, and universally despised by the people who use them daily. But they work, mostly, and replacing them would cost even more millions, so they persist.

**Salesforce** for customer relationship management. What started as a simple database for tracking sales leads has metastasized into a platform with its own programming language (Apex), its own ecosystem, and its own consultants who charge more per hour than therapists.

**ServiceNow** for IT service management. This is how you submit a ticket when your laptop breaks, which then enters a workflow involving approvals and SLAs and routing rules that someone spent months configuring.

**Various Microsoft products** because at some point in the 1990s, someone decided the company was a "Microsoft shop," and now that decision has compounding returns. SharePoint, Teams, Power BI, Azure—if Microsoft sells it, someone in your company is using it, whether it makes sense or not.

**A dozen niche industry-specific applications** that do one thing well but don't talk to anything else. This is why you need middleware. This is why integration is a full-time job.

**A tangle of custom internal applications** written by that senior developer who left three years ago. Nobody knows exactly what they do, but things break when you turn them off, so they keep running on a server in a closet somewhere.

This is the ecosystem. It's not elegant. It's not coherent. It definitely wasn't designed. It evolved, piece by piece, acquisition by acquisition, urgent project by urgent project, until you have the digital equivalent of a medieval city—a maze of narrow passages and dead ends that somehow functions.

## What Enterprise Developers Actually Do

A typical day in enterprise development:

**9:00 AM** - Stand-up meeting. Everyone says what they did yesterday and what they'll do today. Most of it is "still working on that ticket."

**9:15 AM** - You finally look at the ticket you've been assigned. It says "the report is showing wrong numbers." That's it. That's the entire bug description.

**9:30 AM** - You've found the report. It's generated by a stored procedure written in 2009. The stored procedure calls three other stored procedures. One of them uses a view. The view references a table that was deprecated but still has data for some reason.

**11:00 AM** - You've traced through the logic. The "wrong numbers" are actually correct; the user was misinterpreting what the report shows. You update the ticket with a detailed explanation.

**11:15 AM** - The ticket is reopened. "But that's not what we need. Can you change it to show X instead?"

**11:16 AM** - This is now a feature request, not a bug. You escalate to your manager to get approval for the scope change.

**2:00 PM** - After lunch and three meetings, you get approval. You start modifying the stored procedure. You're careful, because this report runs in production, and if you break it, 200 people in the finance department will have nothing to do tomorrow morning.

**3:00 PM** - You've made the change. You test it in the dev environment. It works. You test it in QA. It works. You submit it for code review.

**3:30 PM** - Your coworker points out that your change will break a different report that uses the same stored procedure. You didn't know about that report. Nobody mentioned that report. It's not documented anywhere.

**4:00 PM** - You refactor your change to not break the other report. This is now more complex than you wanted. You submit it for review again.

**Next day** - Your change is deployed to production. The original report now shows correct numbers. The finance department is happy. You get no recognition because this is literally your job.

This is software development in the enterprise. It's not glamorous. It's detective work, archaeology, diplomacy, and careful modification of systems you didn't build and don't fully understand.

## The Eternal Struggle: Build vs. Buy

Every few months, someone has a bright idea: "Instead of using this expensive enterprise software, why don't we just build our own?"

This question has been asked ten thousand times in ten thousand companies. The answer is always the same: because it's harder than it looks.

That expensive enterprise software is expensive because it handles edge cases you haven't thought of. It handles compliance requirements for industries you didn't know existed. It scales to handle the load when your business grows. It has support contracts and SLAs. It has security audits and certification. It has a roadmap and will keep being developed after your internal team moves on to other projects.

Building your own CRM seems simple until you consider: data validation, deduplication, role-based permissions, audit logging, integration with email, integration with calendar, mobile apps, reporting, dashboards, workflow automation, API access, data import/export, compliance with GDPR and CCPA, and oh right, actually managing customer relationships.

So companies buy enterprise software, and then they spend just as much money customizing it to fit their specific needs. The software vendors know this. That's why they have professional services teams. That's why there's a whole ecosystem of consultants.

The dirty secret is that "buy" always becomes "buy and then build a bunch of stuff around it anyway."

## The Sacred Excel Spreadsheet

We need to talk about Excel.

Excel is the most successful piece of software ever created for business. It's not a programming language, officially, but it has variables (cells), functions, conditional logic, loops (via macros), and millions of people building complex applications with it who would never call themselves programmers.

Every company has critical processes running in Excel. Sales forecasts. Budget models. Project timelines. Data analysis. These spreadsheets grow organically, accumulating formulas and macros and dependencies until they become unmaintainable but also irreplaceable.

IT departments hate this. They have no visibility into what these spreadsheets do. They can't audit them, can't back them up properly, can't ensure they're secure. Some executive's laptop gets stolen and suddenly your company's entire pricing strategy is compromised because it was all in a spreadsheet.

So IT tries to move everything into "proper" applications. They build databases and web forms and reporting tools. And users hate them because they're not as flexible as Excel. If you need to add a column in Excel, you add a column. If you need to add a field in the custom application, you submit a ticket, and maybe in six months...

This tension never resolves. Excel persists. The sprawl continues. Programmers shake their heads and go back to writing enterprise applications that will never be as loved as a good spreadsheet.

## Why People Do This For Money

Enterprise software development is not what most CS students dream about. Nobody gets into programming because they're passionate about inventory management systems.

So why do people do it?

**The money is good.** Enterprise developers get paid well because businesses will pay for competent people who can keep their systems running. It's not startup equity or FAANG compensation, but it's steady, predictable, and often comes with excellent benefits.

**The hours are reasonable.** Most enterprise shops don't do crunch time. You work 40 hours, maybe occasionally 45, and then you go home. There's no expectation that you'll be on call at 2 AM unless you're explicitly in ops. You can have a life outside of work.

**The problems are intellectually interesting,** in a specific way. You're not inventing new algorithms, but you're solving complex puzzles. How do you integrate these five systems that were never meant to talk to each other? How do you migrate data from a legacy system without downtime? How do you optimize a report that touches 20 tables and runs for an hour?

**You're helping people.** This is undersold. When you fix that bug in the payroll system, you're ensuring people get paid correctly. When you improve the workflow for processing orders, you're making someone's job less frustrating. It's not saving lives, but it's not nothing, either.

**Job security.** Businesses always need software. They especially need people who understand their existing software. If you become the person who knows how the critical systems work, you're valuable. Perhaps too valuable—there's a whole different problem about being trapped by your own expertise—but you'll always have work.

## The Technical Debt Mountain

Here's what happens in enterprise software:

Year 1: We need a solution quickly. We'll build it the simplest way that works, and we'll clean it up later.

Year 2-5: No time to clean it up; we're adding new features. We'll refactor it someday.

Year 6: The original developers have left. The new people look at the code and say, "We should rebuild this from scratch."

Year 7: The rebuild project starts. It's estimated at 6 months.

Year 8: The rebuild is still in progress. The old system is still in production. Maintenance is split between two systems. Both are suffering.

Year 9: The rebuild is cancelled. Too expensive, too risky. We'll just keep maintaining the old system. We'll replace it someday, probably.

Year 10: The cycle begins again.

This is technical debt in enterprise software. It accumulates faster than it can be paid down, because there's always more urgent work than there is time to do it right.

The smart companies dedicate time to reducing technical debt—maybe 20% of each sprint goes to refactoring, improving tests, updating dependencies. The less smart companies defer all maintenance until something breaks catastrophically, and then they have an emergency.

Most companies are less smart.

## The Art of Legacy Maintenance

The best enterprise developers aren't the ones who write the cleverest code. They're the ones who can read other people's code, understand what it was trying to do, figure out why it's breaking, and fix it without breaking something else.

This requires a specific skill set:

**Patience.** The code is bad. You didn't write it. It's not how you would do it. But it works, mostly, and your job is to make it work better, not to judge.

**Humility.** That "obviously wrong" decision probably made sense at the time, given constraints you don't know about. Before you change something, you need to understand why it exists.

**Pragmatism.** The perfect solution that takes three months is worse than the good-enough solution that ships next week. Business needs trump architectural purity.

**Communication.** You need to translate between business stakeholders who want features and technical constraints. You need to explain why simple requests are complex to implement. You need to negotiate timelines and manage expectations.

**Risk assessment.** Every change might break something. You need to judge whether the risk is worth the benefit, whether you have enough test coverage, whether you should deploy on Friday afternoon (no, you should not).

These skills aren't taught in CS programs. They're learned through painful experience, usually by breaking production at least once.

## The Secret Satisfaction

Here's what nobody tells you about enterprise software development: it can be deeply satisfying.

When you optimize that report and it runs in 30 seconds instead of 30 minutes, and you know that's saving a dozen people an hour of their day, every day—that feels good.

When you automate a manual process that was taking someone three days a month, and now it happens instantly with a button click—that's tangible impact.

When you finally hunt down that intermittent bug that's been haunting production for months, and you find it's a race condition triggered by a specific sequence of events that only happens occasionally—that's intellectual satisfaction on par with solving a difficult puzzle.

When you mentor a junior developer and watch them grow from confused to competent—that's rewarding in a way that has nothing to do with code.

The work itself might not be glamorous, but the impact is real. Businesses run on this software. People's livelihoods depend on it working. That's something.

## The Eternal Enterprise

Enterprise software development will never be cool. It will never be the subject of inspiring biopics. There will never be a conference talk titled "How We Built An Amazing Inventory Management System."

But it will always exist. As long as businesses exist, they will need software. They will need people who can build it, maintain it, and improve it.

Most software developers will spend most of their careers working on enterprise software. This is fine. It's more than fine—it's a perfectly valid way to have a career in software.

You don't have to be passionate about enterprise resource planning. You don't have to dream in SQL. You don't have to love every line of code you write.

You just have to show up, solve problems, and help the business function a little better than it did yesterday.

And at the end of the month, the paycheck clears, which is its own kind of satisfaction.

We write software that pays the bills because, ultimately, we also have bills to pay. That's not selling out. That's not a lack of ambition. That's just being a professional.

The world needs enterprise software developers. It needs them more than it needs the ten-thousandth todo list app or the next social media platform.

So if you're writing stored procedures for a manufacturing company, or maintaining a legacy inventory system, or integrating a CRM with an ERP through a custom middleware layer—know that you're doing real work that matters, even if it's not exciting.

Somebody has to keep the world running.

It might as well be us.

But while we're keeping the visible world running, there's an entire invisible world beneath it—the infrastructure nobody sees, the plumbing that makes everything else possible. That's where we're going next.
