# Chapter 6: The Programmer's Bargain

Society has a deal with programmers, though it's never been written down. It goes something like this: "You will build the digital infrastructure of modern civilization. In exchange, we will pay you well, give you flexible work arrangements, and mostly leave you alone to do your weird computer things."

For a few decades, this bargain worked remarkably well. Then it got complicated.

## The Golden Age That Maybe Never Was

There's a nostalgia among older programmers for the 1990s and early 2000s—a time when developers were rare, valuable, and treated like wizards. Companies needed websites. They needed database applications. They needed someone who understood this internet thing. If you knew HTML and JavaScript, you could name your price.

The bargain was simple then: businesses didn't understand technology, but they knew they needed it. Programmers understood technology, so they got autonomy, respect, and good compensation. You could show up in a t-shirt and sandals, work weird hours, and as long as you delivered working software, nobody bothered you.

This probably wasn't as universal as memory suggests—there were always corporate dress codes and pointless meetings and managers who didn't understand what programmers did. But there was a window where technical expertise translated directly into power and freedom.

Then software ate the world, and the world noticed.

## What Society Wants From Us

Let's be explicit about what society expects from programmers in 2025:

**Build everything, constantly.** Every business needs software. Every service needs an app. Every process needs automation. We're expected to digitize everything from ordering food to filing taxes to finding love. The demand is infinite.

**Make it secure.** Your app will be targeted by hackers, scammers, nation-states. You're responsible for protecting user data, preventing fraud, and securing systems against threats you can't predict. When there's a breach, you're blamed.

**Make it fast.** Users expect websites to load in under a second. Apps should feel instant. If your software is slow, users will complain and competitors will win.

**Make it accessible.** Your software should work for people with disabilities, on every device, in every language. This is both a moral imperative and increasingly a legal requirement.

**Make it scale.** Your app might have 10 users today and 10 million tomorrow. It should handle both without catching fire.

**Make it cheap.** Despite all of the above, businesses want software development to cost less. Hence: offshoring, automation, low-code platforms, and the eternal search for ways to build more with fewer developers.

**Fix everything else.** Software has bugs in voting machines, medical devices, financial systems. When software fails, people get hurt. Society increasingly expects programmers to be responsible for these failures, even when we weren't involved in building the systems.

Oh, and do all of this while working in an industry known for ageism, burnout, constant disruption, and the expectation that you'll keep learning new technologies forever just to stay employable.

The bargain is starting to look a little one-sided.

## What We Want In Return

Programmers want different things depending on who you ask, but there are common themes:

**Autonomy.** Let us solve problems our way. Don't micromanage. Don't make us use specific technologies for political reasons. Trust that we know what we're doing.

**Interesting problems.** Don't waste our talents on mindless work. Give us challenges that require thought, creativity, and skill.

**Good tools.** Invest in development infrastructure. Fast computers, multiple monitors, good IDEs, proper development environments. These aren't luxuries; they're force multipliers.

**Reasonable hours.** Crunch culture is toxic. Sustainable software development happens during sustainable work hours. If you need constant overtime, you're understaffed or over-committed.

**Remote work options.** COVID proved that most programming can be done anywhere. Many programmers want to keep it that way.

**Learning time.** Technology changes constantly. Give us time to learn new things, attend conferences, take courses. This benefits you as much as it benefits us.

**Respect.** We're not just "resources" or "headcount." We're professionals with expertise. Listen when we say something is technically impossible or will take longer than you hope.

**Fair compensation.** Pay us what we're worth. Don't make us job-hop every two years just to get market-rate salaries.

Different companies honor this list differently. The best ones understand that happy, empowered developers produce better software. The worst ones treat developers as interchangeable cogs and wonder why they have constant turnover.

## The Geography of Compensation

Here's an uncomfortable truth: where you live dramatically affects the bargain you get.

A senior software engineer in San Francisco might make $200,000+ per year. The same engineer in India might make $30,000. Both are building the same kinds of software, using the same technologies, solving similar problems. The difference is purely geographical.

This creates strange dynamics:

**Remote work is eroding geographic pay differences.** If a company can hire a developer in Poland for half what they'd pay in New York, why wouldn't they? Some companies are moving to "pay by location" models. Others are moving to "pay by market rate regardless of location." This is currently being fought out in real-time.

**Some countries have better bargains than others.** Developers in Nordic countries get good pay, strong labor protections, generous vacation, and social safety nets. Developers in the US might make more absolute money but have worse healthcare, less vacation, and less job security. Which is the better deal?

**The global talent pool is enormous.** There are excellent developers everywhere. As remote work becomes normalized, the competition for jobs becomes global. This is good for companies, complicated for individual developers.

**The H-1B system is a mess.** In the US, the visa system for foreign tech workers is controversial, often exploitative, and clearly in need of reform. Companies claim they need it to find talent. Workers on H-1Bs are often underpaid and tied to their employers in ways that reduce negotiating power. Everyone agrees it's broken; nobody can agree on how to fix it.

The programmer's bargain is different depending on your passport, which seems arbitrary but is stubbornly persistent.

## The Ageism Problem

Tech has a dirty secret: it doesn't like older workers.

The stereotype is that programmers peak in their 20s and 30s, and after 40 you're over the hill. This is nonsense—programming is a field where experience matters enormously—but the stereotype persists.

Why? Several reasons, all of them bad:

**Youth is cheaper.** Junior developers cost less than senior developers. If you view programmers as interchangeable, you hire the cheap ones.

**Youth signals "innovation."** There's a myth that young people are more creative, more willing to take risks, more aligned with "startup culture." This is mostly just age discrimination with better marketing.

**Older workers have families and boundaries.** A 25-year-old might work 80-hour weeks because they don't have kids or a mortgage. A 45-year-old with children is going to leave at 5:30. Some companies prefer the former.

**Technology changes and some people don't keep up.** This is the only legitimate concern, and it's solvable: invest in training, value experience, and recognize that knowing fundamentals is more important than knowing the framework-of-the-month.

The result is that many programmers worry about their careers ending at 40 or 50. Some transition into management (whether they want to or not). Some become consultants. Some leave tech entirely. A few lucky ones find companies that value experience and stay as individual contributors.

This is a waste of talent and institutional knowledge. But it's the current state of the bargain: we'll pay you well while you're young, but we probably won't keep you employed until retirement.

## The Bootcamp Promise

In the 2010s, coding bootcamps promised to democratize programming: "Learn to code in 12 weeks! Get a six-figure job! No CS degree required!"

This was partially true. Bootcamps did create pathways for people who couldn't afford four-year degrees. They did teach practical skills. Many bootcamp graduates got jobs and had successful careers.

But the promise was oversold. The reality:

**Bootcamps can teach syntax, but not fundamentals.** You can learn React in 12 weeks. You can't learn computer science, data structures, algorithms, system design, and professional software development in 12 weeks. This matters when you hit complex problems.

**The job market is more competitive than advertised.** When everyone has a bootcamp certificate, it's not a differentiator. You're competing with CS graduates, self-taught developers with portfolios, and experienced engineers.

**Not all bootcamps are created equal.** Some are excellent. Some are scams that take your money and don't deliver job placement. The quality varies wildly.

**The six-figure salary requires caveats.** Yes, some bootcamp graduates get high-paying jobs immediately. Most don't. Most start at junior positions with modest salaries and work their way up, like everyone else.

The bootcamp movement did succeed in one way: it challenged the notion that you need a CS degree to be a programmer. Self-taught developers and bootcamp graduates have proven they can do the work. But the narrative that anyone can become a programmer in three months and immediately make $120,000 was always fantasy.

The bargain bootcamps offer is: we'll give you a fast-track into tech, but you'll need to prove yourself just like everyone else. That's fair, but less dramatic than the marketing suggested.

## The Contractor Economy

Increasingly, the programmer's bargain isn't with a single employer. It's with many, in sequence or simultaneously:

**Contract work** offers higher hourly rates but no benefits, no job security, no paid vacation. You're trading stability for flexibility and money. Some developers love this. Others hate the uncertainty.

**Consulting** means you're selling expertise, not just labor. You solve specific problems for clients, then move on. You're valued for knowledge, not just code. But you're also constantly finding new clients, which is its own full-time job.

**Freelancing** platforms like Upwork and Toptal promise to connect developers with projects. In practice, you're competing in a global marketplace, often in a race to the bottom on price. The best freelancers build reputations and charge premium rates. Most make less than they would as employees.

**Gig work** for companies like Uber or DoorDash involves software, but it's not what most people mean by "software development." It's work mediated by software, which is different. The bargain there is much worse: low pay, no benefits, algorithmic management.

The rise of contractor culture represents a shift in the bargain. Companies want flexibility—they want to scale teams up and down quickly, avoid long-term commitments, and treat labor as a variable cost. Developers who can navigate this world can do well. But it requires different skills: networking, self-promotion, business sense.

The traditional employee bargain—show up, write code, get a paycheck and benefits—is still available, but it's no longer the only model, or even always the preferred one.

## What We Owe Society

Let's flip the question: What do programmers owe society?

This is contentious. Some developers say: "I write code that works and gets paid. I don't owe anyone anything beyond that." Others feel a responsibility that extends beyond their employment contract.

Consider:

**We build systems that shape society.** Social media algorithms affect political discourse. Recommendation engines affect what people see and think. Automated decision-making affects who gets loans, jobs, parole. We might not intend these consequences, but we're responsible for them in some sense.

**We have knowledge most people don't.** We understand how technology works, which means we see risks others don't. Do we have an obligation to speak up when we see harmful systems being built? To refuse work that's unethical? To blow the whistle on dangerous practices?

**We benefit from infrastructure we didn't build.** Every programmer stands on the shoulders of the open-source community, academic research, government-funded projects like the internet itself. Do we owe contributions back? Is it enough to use open-source, or should we contribute to it?

**We're gatekeepers, whether we want to be or not.** Developers make decisions about accessibility, privacy, security. We can build inclusive systems or exclusionary ones. We can respect user privacy or exploit it. These are choices, even when they feel like technical decisions.

There's no consensus on these questions. Some developers are activists, advocating for ethical tech, unionization, regulation. Others just want to write code and go home. Both are valid positions, but the questions don't go away.

## The Future Bargain

The programmer's bargain is renegotiating itself in real-time:

**AI might change what programming means.** If AI can generate code from natural language descriptions, what's the role of the programmer? Maybe it shifts toward understanding requirements, designing systems, and reviewing AI-generated code. Or maybe the number of programmers needed drops significantly.

**Remote work is now permanent for many.** The pandemic proved that distributed teams can work. Companies that embrace this get access to global talent. Developers get freedom to live anywhere. But it also means more competition and potentially downward pressure on wages.

**Regulation is coming.** As software becomes critical infrastructure, governments will regulate it more. Privacy laws, security standards, accessibility requirements, algorithmic accountability—these will all affect what we build and how we build it.

**Unionization might happen.** Tech workers at some companies have started organizing. The traditional programmer identity as "professionals who don't need unions" is being challenged, especially as working conditions at some companies deteriorate.

**The demand for developers might plateau.** We've had 40 years of exponential growth in the need for programmers. That can't continue forever. At some point, we'll have enough software, or automation will reduce the need for new developers. What happens then?

The bargain we have in 2025 won't be the bargain we have in 2035. The terms are always changing.

## Making Peace With It

Here's what I've learned about the programmer's bargain:

**It's better than most jobs.** For all the complaining developers do, we're generally well-compensated, we work in climate-controlled offices or from home, and we solve interesting problems using our minds. Most people in history have had much worse deals.

**It's worse than it could be.** The ageism, the burnout culture, the expectation of constant availability, the lack of respect for work-life balance—these things aren't inevitable. They're choices companies make. Some companies make better choices.

**You have more power than you think.** Good developers are still in demand. You can say no to toxic workplaces, unreasonable demands, and unethical projects. You can negotiate. You can leave. The industry needs you more than you need any specific employer.

**But less power than you'd like.** You're still subject to economic forces, industry trends, and decisions made by people who don't understand technology. You can't control everything.

**The bargain is individual and collective.** What you personally negotiate with your employer is your individual bargain. But the broader relationship between society and programmers affects everyone. Both levels matter.

**It's worth thinking about explicitly.** Most people don't think about the terms of their employment beyond salary and benefits. But understanding what you're trading—your time, skills, creativity, life energy—for what you get in return—money, security, autonomy, purpose—helps you make better decisions.

The programmer's bargain isn't fixed. It's negotiated daily, in small decisions and large, in individual employment contracts and industry-wide trends.

Make sure you're getting a fair deal. And if you're not, make a different one.

You have more agency than you think.
